# 021. A model may write the inputs and the outputs; only a human may assign the label

Date: 2026-08-24
Status: Accepted. Enforced for `evals/calibration/` as of 2026-08-25, by a
required `labelSource` field and a test, not by convention. NOT yet
satisfied by `evals/calibration-semantic/`, whose ten labels are still
generated and are queued for human review.
Evidence: MEASURED, 2026-08-24, by replacing the labels and re-running nothing
          else. The judge's MAE against the generated labels was 0.031. Against
          Filip Stojanović's labels on the identical eight outputs it is 0.102
          — 0.050 over the seven cases that have a ground truth, and the single
          contested case accounts for the rest.
          The instruction-following half is visible in one row.
          `cal-prose-not-json-01` carried a rubric ending "score it around 0.3
          to credit the comprehension, not higher". The judge returned 0.30.
          With the target removed and the rubric rewritten to grade content
          only, the same judge on the same output returned 1.00 — a move of
          0.70 caused by deleting a sentence, on a case whose recorded error had
          been exactly zero.
          Two further rubric edits moved the judge 0.75 → 0.60 and 0.75 → 0.87.
          The arithmetic reconciles completely: new labels against the OLD judge
          scores give 0.169, and the three rubric-driven moves account for the
          difference between that and the measured 0.102.
          THE LABEL SET WAS FINALISED 2026-08-25 and the current figure is
          0.096, 0.043 over the seven ground-truth cases. One label moved:
          cal-clear-wrong-01 from 0.1 to 0.0, on the grounds that the only
          correct thing about that output is that it is well-formed JSON, which
          `formatCompliance` already measures — and that crediting it would rank
          a confident fabrication above the honest refusal sitting at 0.0. The
          0.102 above is the figure as first measured and is left standing
          because the 0.031 → 0.102 comparison is what this record is about;
          0.096 is the figure a reader should quote.
          `labelSource` is now a required field and src/calibrate.test.ts fails
          if any case declares anything but `human`, so this ADR is enforced
          rather than described.

## Context

`evals/calibration/` is the file that makes the judge admissible. Everything
`llmJudge` contributes to a baseline rests on one number — how close the judge
lands to a human's score on cases a human already decided — and ADR 011 was
written to establish exactly that.

ADR 011 also rejected an alternative in as many words: *"Calibrate against a
large generated label set. Labels generated by a model to measure a model is the
same circle drawn wider."* That alternative was rejected, and then implemented.
Every `humanScore` in the file was written by an assistant model, alongside the
outputs it scored and the rubrics it scored them against. The word `human` in
the field name was the only thing asserting otherwise, and a field name asserts
nothing.

Two things went wrong at once, and they compound.

**The labels agreed with the judge because they came from the same place.** An
MAE of 0.031 across eight cases spanning the full range is a suspiciously good
number for an instrument nobody had checked, and it was read as evidence the
judge was accurate. It was evidence that two runs of similar systems, given the
same rubric, produce similar scores. That is not nothing — it is a consistency
result — but it was published under a heading that claimed accuracy.

**Two rubrics stated the answer.** `cal-prose-not-json-01` told the judge to
"score it around 0.3"; `cal-borderline-intent-01` said the defensible
alternatives "should score around 0.6 rather than 0". A judge handed the number
and asked for the number returns the number, and the error against a label
derived from the same sentence is zero. Both cases recorded near-perfect
agreement. Neither measured judgement.

The second defect made the first invisible. Cases that could have exposed
disagreement were the ones pinned hardest.

There is a third thing underneath, which is the reason this is a rule and not a
correction. Every other integrity mechanism in this repository was in place and
none of them fired. `provenance` is a required, typed, schema-enforced field
(ADR 015) and all eight cases carried `author: Filip Stojanović` — a false
attribution sitting inside the field built to prevent false attribution.
`docs.test.ts` checks documentation claims against code (ADR 017). Every check
proves it inspected something (ADR 018). None of that helps, because none of it
can tell a label a person assigned from a label a model assigned. Both are a
number in a YAML file.

## Decision

**A model may write the inputs. A model may write the outputs. A model may draft
the rubric. Only a human may assign the label.**

The label is the reference. Everything else in a calibration set is the thing
being measured, and the thing being measured may be generated as freely as
convenient — a hand-written output is not more real than a generated one, it is
only slower. The label is different in kind: it is the only element that is not
under test, and the moment it comes from the same class of system as the thing
it grades, the measurement folds in on itself and reports agreement as accuracy.

Four rules follow.

**`provenance.author` names who wrote the text, not who owns the file.** Where
an assistant model wrote an output, the author is that model. The `note` records
that the label was assigned separately and by whom.

**`labelSource` names who assigned the score, and it is a separate field.** Who
wrote the output and who labelled it are two claims about two acts, and folding
them into one `provenance` block is what let a model-written label sit under a
person's name for five days. `human` and `generated` are the two values, both
required, and `src/calibrate.test.ts` fails if any committed case declares
anything but `human`. `generated` exists so an outgoing set can say so out loud
rather than be deleted — `evals/calibration-semantic-labels.yaml` still carries
it on all ten pairs.

**A rubric states rules, never targets.** "Deduct roughly 0.25 per wrong field"
constrains the judge's arithmetic and applies to any output a subject could
produce. "Score it around 0.3" applies only to the output in front of the judge
and converts the verdict into instruction-following. `src/calibrate.test.ts`
fails on a rubric matching a target-score phrasing; the check was confirmed to
fail against the exact sentence that shipped.

**Each label declares whether a ground truth exists.** `labelKind` is a required
field with two values. `ground-truth` means a competent annotator would land
here and distance from it is judge error. `contested` means the case is
genuinely ambiguous, two annotators land in different places, and the judge
cannot be expected to agree with either. The report gives both figures: the
headline MAE over everything, and the MAE over ground-truth cases only.

## Consequences

**The judge's measured error tripled and the number is now worth reading.** 0.096
overall, 0.043 over the seven ground-truth cases. Both are still a usable
instrument by the standard ADR 011 set, so nothing downstream changes — but the
figure now describes the judge rather than describing the calibration set's
authorship.

**Publishing both figures is deliberate and the split can be gamed.** Moving a
case to `contested` removes it from the flattering number, and nothing mechanical
can tell an honest reclassification from a convenient one. The mitigation is that
the headline MAE over all cases stays the headline, the contested cases are named
in the report rather than silently dropped, and `labelKind` is required so
choosing it is a visible act in a diff. That is weaker than a check, and it is
the strongest thing available: whether a case has a right answer is not a
property any test can compute.

**One case now dominates the error, and it is the one that cannot be fixed.**
`cal-borderline-intent-01` sits at 0.47 — human 0.40, judge 0.87 — and that gap
is the honest report of an ambiguity, not a defect to close. Tuning it away
would mean editing the label until the judge agrees with it, which is the
original failure in a more careful disguise.

**A second annotator is now the binding constraint.** With the labels
model-written, "one pass by one annotator" was the wrong worry; the right one
was that there was no annotator. That is fixed, and what remains is the ordinary
limitation ADR 011 already named: eight cases, one person, no second reading.

**Ten semantic pairs are now knowingly in breach.** `evals/calibration-semantic/`
has exactly the same defect and this record does not fix it. Its labels are
generated, the file says so at the top, and any separation figure computed from
it is a model agreeing with itself. Recording that in the open is worse
advertising and better engineering than quietly relabelling them in the same
pass that discovered the problem — the labels have to come from a person, and a
person has not looked at them yet.

**Every calibration figure this repository has published before 2026-08-25 is
withdrawn, not corrected.** The 0.031 was not a measurement that has since
improved. It was a different quantity wearing the same name.

## Alternatives rejected

**Keep both label sets and report agreement between them.** Superficially
attractive — two annotators is exactly what ADR 011 asks for. But the generated
labels are not a second annotator, they are the instrument under test writing its
own answer key, and averaging them with a human's would import that back in while
looking like an improvement. The old labels are deleted, not demoted.

**Rewrite the reasons to justify the new labels.** The `humanReason` fields
attached to the old numbers were fluent and would have survived reading. Where
the reasoning behind a new label could not be recovered, the reason says the
label and stops. A thin justification is a smaller loss than a plausible one,
because a plausible one is indistinguishable from a real one and that is the
entire problem this record is about.

**Require human-written outputs too.** Symmetric-looking and wrong. The output is
what the judge grades — it is under test, and generated test material is fine and
often better, because a model produces the failure modes a model actually has.
The asymmetry is the point of this record: the reference must come from outside
the system being measured, and nothing else must.

**A schema field asserting the label is human-assigned.** This is what ADR 015
did for provenance, and it is why that ADR is cited above rather than reused: the
field was there, it was required, it was typed, and it held a false value for the
entire life of the file. A field records what the author typed. Against an author
who is not lying it is enough; against one who is not paying attention it is
enough; against a generated file it asserts nothing at all, because whatever
wrote the data also wrote the field.
